How to find a reputable book editor (without getting burned)

A practical process for finding a book editor: samples, scopes, red flags, genres, contracts, and when marketplace listings are enough.

Finding an editor is less “hunt for a unicorn” and more “run a short professional hiring process.” The authors who get burned usually skip samples and scopes. The authors who get a book they can still claim run the same six checks every time: stage, genre fit, sample pages, written scope, payment terms, and a clean exit if the chemistry is wrong.

This is not a personality contest. It is a purchase of judgment. You are hiring someone to put cold light on the page—and to stay warm with you while you decide what to do about it.

1. Name the stage

If you cannot name the pass, you cannot hire it. “I just need an editor” is how people buy proofreading for a first draft, or a developmental edit for a file that only needed commas. Read types of editing first if the labels still blur.

  • Manuscript critique — a diagnostic letter: what is working, what is costing readers, which three revision moves matter most. No full copy markup.
  • Developmental editing — structure, character, pacing, argument, reader promise. The book as a reading experience.
  • Copyediting — grammar, clarity, continuity, style sheet. Sentences, not story surgery.
  • Proofreading — residual errors after the text (and often the layout) is frozen.
  • Formatting — production craft, not editorial judgment. Different job, different hire.

Still deciding between a letter and a deep structural pass? See critique vs developmental. Timing the hire so you do not pay twice: when to hire an editor.

2. Shortlist for genre fit

Editors are not interchangeable instruments. Romance, thriller, memoir, literary fiction, and epic fantasy fail in different ways. An editor who has never sat with category beats will “fix” a meet-cute into a character study. An editor allergic to lyric will sand the one thing your memoir has going for it.

Ask for recent comparable titles—or a redacted sample letter in your category. Redacted is fine. Vague is not. “I edit all genres” often means “I have opinions about sentences.” That is copyediting energy, not developmental energy.

Fit is also temperament. You want someone who can tell you the middle sags without treating you like a defective appliance. Warmth without rigor is a writing group. Rigor without warmth is a demolition.

3. Always get sample pages

Chemistry and clarity of comments matter more than a pretty website. Pay for a short sample if needed—ten to twenty pages, or a chapter plus a stretch from the middle, is enough to see how they think.

When you read the sample, ignore the volume of markup. Look for:

  • Whether they named the actual problem (stakes, causality, viewpoint leak) or only decorated the sentences
  • Whether queries sound like a collaborator or a hall monitor
  • Whether your voice is still on the page after their suggestions
  • Whether they admitted what they would not touch at this stage

Two or three samples beat a spreadsheet of rates. One complimentary sample from a stranger can still be a sales document. A paid sample with a written mini-scope is closer to the work you will actually buy.

4. Demand a written scope

If it is not on the page, it is not in the job. A reputable editor will put the following in writing before you transfer money:

  • Deliverables — editorial letter? tracked changes? style sheet? a call? how many manuscript comments?
  • Stage and exclusions — what this pass will not do (no plot overhaul inside a copyedit; no line polish inside a critique)
  • Timeline — start date, delivery date, and how they handle queues
  • Communication — email only, one midpoint check-in, or a debrief call
  • Price — per-word or flat, currency, what happens if word count changes midstream
  • Revision rounds — included or not; what “one round” actually means
  • Payment schedule — deposit, milestone, remainder on delivery. Never 100% upfront with no paper.

Educational market ranges (not Polar Bear quotes) typically land around $0.008–$0.018/word for a critique, $0.03–$0.06 for developmental, $0.018–$0.035 for copyediting, $0.01–$0.02 for proofreading, and $0.045–$0.08 for a developmental-plus-copy package. Run your word count through the editing cost calculator, then compare that ballpark to the written scope—not to a listing title that says “full edit.”

5. Marketplace vs boutique

Marketplaces are directories with payment rails. Boutiques are desks with a point of view. Neither is a moral category. They fail in different ways.

Marketplaces (including well-known freelancer platforms such as Reedsy) can be useful when you want reviews, escrow, and a wide menu of specialists. You still have to run this hiring process. A five-star badge does not name your stage, prove genre fit, or write your scope. High-volume mills and gig listings are worse: rates that only make sense if the manuscript is barely read, and labels that call a spellcheck a “developmental edit.”

Boutique studios (this desk included) trade breadth for continuity: one house style, a style sheet that survives sequels, and someone who will tell you not to buy the wrong pass. The risk is fit, not fraud—so the sample still matters. Directories from professional associations can also be a sourcing channel; treat them as phone books, not endorsements.

Choose a marketplace when you need a specialist you cannot otherwise find, and you are willing to project-manage the hire. Choose a boutique when you want a relationship, a written process, and a no that protects your budget. Polar Bear’s process is the same either way you arrived: fit check → sample pages → written scope → delivery and debrief.

6. NDAs and ordinary confidentiality

Most trade manuscripts do not need a separate non-disclosure agreement. Reputable editors already treat files as confidential: no sharing pages, no training public models on your draft, no “look what I edited” posts with identifiable excerpts. That duty should appear in the agreement or terms—not as a vibe.

Request a formal NDA when the material is actually sensitive: an unannounced franchise or tie-in, a high-profile memoir, corporate or legal exposure, or a project under a publisher’s existing confidentiality clause. Keep the NDA specific (what is confidential, for how long, what is excluded). A twelve-page rider that forbids the editor from listing the genre on an invoice is not professionalism; it is delay.

Do not let an NDA become a stalling tactic—yours or theirs. If ordinary confidentiality is already in the scope, a paid sample should not wait on legal theater.

Red flags

Walk away, or slow way down, if you see any of these:

  • Guarantees of agents, traditional deals, bestseller lists, or “we’ll make it viral.” Nobody honest can sell that, and we will not pretend to.
  • No sample, no contract, and pressure to pay 100% upfront with no milestones
  • Rates that only make sense if the manuscript is barely read—especially “developmental” at proofreading prices
  • Confusing proofreading with developmental work, or offering one vague “full edit” that claims to do both in a weekend
  • A sample that rewrites your voice into generic workshop prose
  • Unwillingness to put deliverables, exclusions, and a delivery date in writing
  • Disappearing communication during the quoting stage (it does not improve after the deposit)

What to send in the first email

Save both of you a round-trip. Include:

  • Genre and two or three comparable titles
  • Word count and a one-paragraph premise (not the query letter, the book)
  • Stage: draft two, post-beta, post-dev, pre-upload
  • Deadline, including the honest one
  • What you already know is weak
  • Whether you want a sample and which file you can send

Then wait for a human answer that names a pass. If they quote a number before they name a stage, they are selling a product, not reading a manuscript.

FAQ

Should I hire the cheapest editor?

Hire the clearest editor for your stage. Cheap wrong-stage work is the most expensive option. A bargain proofread of a structurally broken novel still leaves you with a structurally broken novel.

Do I need an NDA?

Most trade manuscripts do not require one. Reputable editors treat files as confidential by default. Request an NDA when the material is legally sensitive—unannounced franchise work, high-profile memoir, or corporate IP. Do not let an NDA delay a paid sample if ordinary confidentiality is already in the agreement.

How many editors should I sample?

Two or three is enough. One sample can mislead you; five samples usually means you are stalling. Pay for short samples when they are not complimentary, and compare the quality of comments—not just the density of red ink.

Is a marketplace safer than a boutique studio?

Neither is automatically safer. Marketplaces add reviews and payment rails; you still have to check genre fit, sample pages, and written scope. A boutique relationship is often better for sequels, style-sheet continuity, and a desk that will tell you not to buy the wrong pass.

What if the sample rewrite flattens my voice?

Walk away. A good sample should make the page cleaner or clearer without sanding the author off it. Ask how they handle dialect, rhythm, and intentional fragments. Voice protection is a skill, not a slogan.

Should I hire an editor before the draft is finished?

Almost never for a full manuscript pass. Sample-chapter coaching exists, but developmental, copy, and proof all assume a complete file. Hire after you have revised as far as you can see; otherwise you pay to edit pages you will delete.

How do I know an editor is a genre fit?

Ask for recent comparable titles or a redacted sample letter in your category. Romance, thriller, memoir, and epic fantasy fail in different ways. An editor who loves literary quietude may be the wrong reader for a category romance—and vice versa.
