How much does copyediting a book cost?

Educational ranges of $0.018–$0.035 per word, with 60k / 80k / 100k tables and a clear contrast against proofreading and line editing—so you buy cleanliness, not a mislabeled last pass.

Copyediting cost is the number most self-publishing authors actually need, once the story stands. It is also the number most often confused with proofreading (cheaper, later, thinner) and with line editing (more stylistic, sometimes bundled, sometimes not). If you remember one thing from this page: copyediting is the professional language-and-consistency pass you buy after structure is locked and before you format. It is not a bargain-bin proofread, and it is not a developmental rewrite wearing a grammar hat.

The ranges below are USD market ballparks for trade-length manuscripts, matching the bands in Polar Bear’s cost tools. They are educational, not a Polar Bear quote. Run your own length through the editing cost calculator, then read the wider stack in how much book editing costs.

Educational ranges ($0.018–$0.035 per word)

Independent copyeditors commonly quote between about 1.8 and 3.5 cents per word for book-length work. The low end tends to mean a lighter mechanical pass on a clean, already-revised manuscript: grammar, punctuation, spelling, obvious continuity. The high end tends to mean denser intervention—more recasting for clarity, more continuity work, a heavier style sheet, or a draft that still fights the reader at the sentence level without needing structural surgery.

Keep the 80,000-word row in your head: roughly $1,440–$2,800 for a genuine copyedit. That is less than a typical developmental pass on the same book, and more than a proofread—which is exactly where this service belongs in the stack. A 60k novella or tight category novel sits on the first row. A 100k thriller or fantasy sits on the third, and it will take longer per thousand words if names, magic systems, or timelines need tracking.

What a copyedit actually covers

Copyediting is where professionalism becomes invisible. Readers should not notice the work—only that the prose is clean, consistent, and trustworthy. In practice that means grammar, usage, punctuation, and syntax; clarity and concision without flattening voice; continuity of names, dates, eye color, and world rules; and a project style sheet you can hand to a proofreader or reuse on a sequel.

What it does not cover: plot overhauls, chapter reordering, “make this more commercial,” or a last typo hunt on designed pages. Plot is developmental editing. Typos after layout are proofreading. Buying copyediting too early—while you are still moving scenes—means you will pay again when the new sentences arrive. That is the expensive version of this otherwise reasonable number.

Deliverables to insist on in writing: a tracked-changes manuscript, a style sheet, and query comments where authorial choice is required. If a quote is silent on those, the rate is not comparable to a professional pass, no matter how friendly the per-word figure looks.

Copyediting vs proofreading vs line editing

Three labels. Three jobs. Authors collapse them because all three touch sentences. The cost difference is the tell.

Proofreading is not a cheaper copyedit. It is a different pass on a frozen file. At $0.01–$0.02 per word, an 80k proofread often sits around $800–$1,600. That is a last error hunt: residual typos, layout glitches, headers, and the mistakes that sneak in when you accept tracked changes. It will not build your style sheet or recast a muddy paragraph.

Line editing sits beside copyediting, not below it. The line editor asks whether the prose sings and moves—rhythm, repetition, tonal slips, paragraph propulsion. The copyeditor asks whether it is correct and consistent. Many professional passes blend both; labels vary. The practical move is to ask what percentage of the work is stylistic versus mechanical, then compare a sample. The comparison guide is line editing vs copyediting.

If the story itself still sags, none of these three is the first spend. That is developmental work, which typically costs more because the unit of attention is the book. See developmental editing cost when you are pricing structure rather than sentences.

What moves copyediting cost

  • Word count — an honest count in the file you will send, including front matter you want edited.
  • Manuscript condition — a twice-revised, beta-tested novel is faster than a draft still discovering its tense and viewpoint.
  • How much line work is bundled — a copyedit that also reshapes voice is not the same job as a mechanical cleanup.
  • Continuity load — casts, maps, magic systems, and dual timelines add tracking time.
  • Timeline — a rush is a surcharge, not a favor. Book the slot while the developmental revision is still underway.

Two 80k novels can sit at opposite ends of $0.018–$0.035 for boring, legitimate reasons. Compare sample pages. Compare the style sheet. Compare whether queries are included. Rate-only shopping is how authors buy a proofread labeled as a copyedit and then wonder why the sentences still lurch.

How to spend this number well

  1. Lock structure first. If betas still argue about the ending, wait. See when to hire an editor.
  2. Implement developmental or critique revisions before the language pass. Copyediting a draft you are about to gut is a donation, not a strategy.
  3. Budget the 60k / 80k / 100k row that matches your file, then keep a smaller line for proofreading after format.
  4. Ask for a short paid sample if you have not worked with the editor. Chemistry on queries matters as much as comma policy.
  5. Put deliverables in the scope: tracked changes, style sheet, timeline, and what happens if word count changes.

FAQ

How much does it cost to copyedit a novel?

Educational market ranges for book copyediting typically sit around $0.018–$0.035 per word. That puts a 60,000-word manuscript near $1,080–$2,100, an 80,000-word novel near $1,440–$2,800, and a 100,000-word book near $1,800–$3,500. Heavy line work, a messy draft, or a rush can sit higher. These figures are illustrations, not a Polar Bear quote.

What is the difference between copyediting cost and proofreading cost?

Proofreading is cheaper because it is a last error hunt on stable text, often after layout—typically about $0.01–$0.02 per word. Copyediting is a language-and-consistency pass while the file can still flex: grammar, clarity, continuity, and a style sheet, typically $0.018–$0.035 per word. Buying proofreading as a cheap substitute for copyediting leaves professional problems in the sentences.

Is line editing more expensive than copyediting?

Often, yes, when it is scoped as a true style pass. Line editing reshapes rhythm, voice, and paragraph flow; copyediting protects correctness and consistency. Many combined passes exist. Ask what share of the work is stylistic versus mechanical, and request sample pages so you are not paying a copyediting rate for line-level surgery—or a line rate for mechanical cleanup.

Can I skip copyediting and only pay for proofreading?

Only if the language is already professionally clean and the content is frozen. Proofreading catches residual typos; it does not build a style sheet, resolve continuity, or recast unclear sentences. If you have never had a professional language pass, proofreading alone is usually the wrong stage.

Should I copyedit before or after formatting?

Copyedit first, on the manuscript. Format after the language pass is implemented. Proofread on the near-final interior—print and ebook—so layout artifacts get caught. Copyediting a designed file is slower and more expensive than it needs to be.

Does a copyedit include a style sheet?

A professional copyedit should. The style sheet records hyphenation, capitalization, spelling variants, character names, and world rules so sequels and a later proofreader are not guessing. If a quote does not mention a style sheet, ask. It is part of the value, not an extra flourish.
