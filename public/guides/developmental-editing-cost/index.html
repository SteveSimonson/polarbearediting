How much does developmental editing cost for a novel?

Educational market ranges of $0.03–$0.06 per word, with 60k / 80k / 100k tables and a clear contrast against copyediting—so you budget the right pass, not the cheapest label.

Authors searching for developmental editing cost are rarely shopping for a number. They are trying to decide whether the big-picture pass is worth a car payment. The honest answer: a genuine structural edit of a novel is one of the most expensive editorial services because it is also the highest-leverage one. You are not buying commas. You are buying cold light on whether the book works.

The figures below are industry-style ballparks in USD for trade-length fiction, aligned with the ranges we use across Polar Bear’s cost tools. They are educational, not a Polar Bear quote. Educational ranges for novels ($0.03–$0.06 per word)

Most independent developmental editors working on novels quote in the neighborhood of three to six cents per word. At the low end you are usually buying a thorough read, a substantial editorial letter, and selective comments. At the high end you are buying slower, more granular collaboration—denser in-manuscript notes, a tighter revision roadmap, and more time spent on a complicated plot or multi-viewpoint design.

An 80,000-word novel is the planning number most authors should memorize: roughly $2,400–$4,800 for a real developmental pass. Shorter category romance or taut literary novels sit closer to the 60k row. Epic fantasy, sprawling historicals, and dual-timeline thrillers often land on or above the 100k row—and they take longer per thousand words because the editor is tracking more machinery.

What you are actually paying for

Developmental editing asks whether the book holds as a reading experience. Plot causality. Character desire and agency. Pacing across acts, not just inside a scene. Point of view leaks. Promise and payoff. Theme that feels chosen rather than accidental. For memoir and narrative nonfiction the same pass looks at argument, chronology, and whether the personal story earns the claim it is making.

Typical deliverables: a prioritized editorial letter, a revision sequence so you do not rebuild chapter four before you have decided the ending, and—when scoped—comments on structural moments in the manuscript. That is slow work. An editor cannot skim 80,000 words, hold the whole architecture in mind, and write useful guidance in an afternoon. The rate looks high until you divide it by the hours of attention a novel actually consumes.

What you are not paying for: a rewrite in the editor’s voice, a guaranteed agent, a bestseller ranking, or a promise that readers will buy. Cold light on the page. Warmth for the writer. That is the job.

Developmental cost vs copyediting cost

This is where budgets go wrong. Copyediting is cheaper per word because the unit of attention is the sentence, not the book. If the structure still moves—if you are still debating endings, cutting subplots, or changing who the antagonist is—copyediting is a polish you will pay for twice. If the story already lands and the sentences need professional cleanliness, copyediting is the correct spend and developmental work would be overbuying.

If you only remember one contrast: developmental editing is expensive because it changes what the book is. Copyediting is cheaper because it changes how the book reads once the architecture stands.

What moves the number

  • Word count — still the primary lever. A 100k manuscript is not “a bit longer than 80k”; it is twenty-five percent more book to hold.
  • Draft condition — a twice-revised novel after betas costs less attention per page than a first draft that still discovers itself in chapter twelve.
  • Genre complexity — multi-POV epic fantasy, dual timelines, and specialized historical worlds take longer than a single-viewpoint contemporary novel.
  • Depth of intervention — letter-only guidance is not the same as letter plus dense manuscript markup plus a follow-up pass on the revision plan.
  • Timeline — rush fees are real. Planning a slot is cheaper than asking someone to drop a queue for your launch date.

None of those variables is a trick. They are why two 80,000-word novels can sit at opposite ends of the same band. Compare sample pages and a written scope, not sticker rates in isolation.

When a cheaper pass is the smarter spend

If you cannot yet afford the 80k developmental row, do not buy a fake version of it. Buy a manuscript critique instead: a diagnostic letter, a ranked problem list, and a fit test with the editor. Critique rates typically sit well below full developmental work. You still get professional eyes on structure; you do not get the same density of in-manuscript collaboration.

The other cheaper pass that is sometimes correct: skip developmental work entirely. If trusted readers already finish the book, agree on what it is about, and only trip over language, you want copyediting—not a structural excavation. Our when to hire an editor guide is the timing map; this page is the price of getting the timing right.

What is never cheaper: paying for copyediting on a draft that still needs chapter surgery, then paying for developmental work, then paying to copyedit again. Wrong-stage spending is how a $1,800 language pass becomes a $6,000 loop.

How to budget without guessing

  1. Count words in the actual file, not the file you hope to cut to.
  2. Name the stage. Structure still moving? Price developmental or a critique first.
  3. Run the 60k / 80k / 100k math through the cost calculator so the range is visible, not mythical.
  4. Reserve a second tranche for copyedit and proof after you revise. Developmental work creates a new draft; that draft still needs language care.
  5. Request sample pages and a written scope before you commit to the high end of any band.

FAQ

Is $0.01 per word realistic for developmental editing?

Usually no, not for a genuine structural edit of a novel. A true developmental pass requires reading the whole book more than once, writing a prioritized letter, and mapping revisions. Ultra-low rates often mean a skim, a mislabeled copyedit, or work that has been outsourced without a named editor. Ask for sample pages and a written list of deliverables.

Why is developmental editing more expensive than copyediting?

Copyediting works at the sentence. Developmental editing works at the book: plot logic, character agency, pacing, point of view, and whether the promise to the reader is paid. That takes slower reading, more thinking, and a longer editorial letter. On typical market bands, copyediting sits around $0.018–$0.035 per word; developmental work sits around $0.03–$0.06.

Can I get a cheaper manuscript critique instead of a full developmental edit?

Yes, and it is often the smarter first spend. A critique diagnoses; a developmental edit collaborates on the revision. If you need direction, a fit test, or a ranked list of problems, start with a critique. Choose full developmental work when you already know the structure needs surgery and you want granular guidance through the rewrite.

How much does developmental editing cost for 60,000, 80,000, or 100,000 words?

At educational rates of $0.03–$0.06 per word, a 60,000-word novel often lands around $1,800–$3,600; 80,000 words around $2,400–$4,800; and 100,000 words around $3,000–$6,000. Complex multi-POV work, a rough draft, or a rush timeline can sit above those bands. These are market illustrations, not a Polar Bear quote.

Should I hire a developmental editor before or after beta readers?

After a serious self-revision, and usually after beta readers. Betas tell you where civilians stall. A developmental editor interprets those stalls, ranks them, and gives you a revision order so you do not rewrite in circles. Hiring before you have revised on your own often means paying someone to find problems you could have caught.

Does a developmental editor rewrite my novel?

No. A reputable developmental editor guides decisions, models solutions, and marks structural moments. The pages stay yours. If someone offers to ghostwrite the book under an editing label, that is a different service with a different contract.
