Hire a book editor for self-publishing

You do not need a catalog of strangers. You need the right pass, a written scope, and an editor who will tell you if you are not ready to spend yet.

If you are ready to hire a book editor, you are past the curiosity stage. The manuscript exists. The question is who touches it, at which depth, and through which kind of shop. This page is the conversion map: boutique versus marketplace, what to send, what Polar Bear actually does, and how to keep the hire from becoming a rewrite loop. For the step-by-step search process—samples, red flags, contracts in more detail—use how to find a book editor. That guide is the hunt. This one is the decision.

Polar Bear Editing works with indie, hybrid, and pre-agent authors on fiction and memoir. We do not promise traditional deals, bestseller placement, or sales. We promise cold light on the page and a scope you can read without a decoder ring.

Hire for a stage, not for “an editor”

The most expensive hire is the wrong-stage hire. “I need an editor” is how travelers say “I need a ticket” without naming a destination. Before you send money, name the pass:

  • Developmental — the book as a whole: plot, character, pacing, argument. After you have revised hard, often after betas. See developmental editing.
  • Critique — a diagnostic letter when you need direction more than full markup. See manuscript critique.
  • Copyediting — language, continuity, style sheet. After structure is locked. See copyediting.
  • Proofreading — last error hunt, preferably after layout. See proofreading.

If you cannot name the stage, start with types of editing and when to hire an editor. Hiring Polar Bear (or anyone) before that clarity just buys a confident-sounding guess. We would rather tell you to revise once more than take a copyedit on a draft that still needs chapter surgery.

Boutique shop vs marketplace listing

Self-publishing authors usually choose between two storefronts: a small editorial practice, or a marketplace that lists many freelancers. Neither is a moral category. They are different ways of buying attention.

Boutique editor or shop Marketplace or large agency
What you are buyingA named process and an ongoing relationshipA catalog of providers and a platform workflow
Best whenYou want one editorial home and honest stage adviceYou already know the pass you need and can brief well
Watch-outsCapacity and waitlists; not a 24-hour storefrontVariable quality; labels that do not match the work
Still requiredSample pages and a written scopeSample pages and a written scope

Marketplaces such as Reedsy can be a useful directory when you want to compare several freelancers in one interface. High-volume agencies and services such as Scribendi can be a fit for a tightly defined language pass if you know exactly what you are ordering. The risk is not that those businesses exist. The risk is treating a star rating as a substitute for sample pages, or assuming “editing” on a listing page means developmental work when the editor is actually proofreading.

A boutique practice—Polar Bear is one—trades catalog size for continuity. You talk to the people who will read the book. You get a recommendation that may be “not yet,” which is a terrible marketplace upsell and a decent editorial act. You also get the constraint of a small shop: we cannot absorb every rush, every genre, or every word count at once. If you need a dozen quotes by Friday from strangers, a marketplace is built for that. If you want one editorial relationship through critique, revision, copyedit, and proof, a boutique is built for that.

Polar Bear approach: fit check → sample pages → written scope → delivery and debrief. No guaranteed sales. No invented team. A human edit with a paper trail.

What hiring Polar Bear looks like

  1. You write. Genre, word count, deadline if it is real, and where the manuscript stands (draft two, post-beta, story locked, pre-upload). Attach or offer sample pages.
  2. We name the stage. Sometimes that is developmental work. Sometimes it is a critique. Sometimes it is copyediting. Sometimes it is “revise, then come back.” The recommendation is the first edit.
  3. Sample pages, if we have not worked together. Chemistry on comments matters more than a pretty homepage. You should recognize your book in the margin notes.
  4. A written scope. Deliverables, timeline, price, what happens if the word count moves, and whether any follow-up is included.
  5. The work, then a debrief. You should leave knowing what to do next, not only what was wrong.

That path lives on the contact page. It is a real inquiry, not a newsletter signup. If the fit is wrong—academic monograph, technical documentation, a genre we do not serve well—we will say so rather than stretch the brand.

What it costs to hire (educational, not a quote)

Budget two lines, not one miracle pass. Story-stage work (critique or developmental) and language-stage work (copyedit, then proof) are different spends. Educational market bands we publish elsewhere: developmental editing often sits around $0.03–$0.06 per word; copyediting around $0.018–$0.035; proofreading around $0.01–$0.02. An 80,000-word novel is the planning number most authors need.

Work the length through the editing cost calculator, then read how much book editing costs, plus the deeper tables in developmental editing cost and copyediting cost. Those pages are for planning. A Polar Bear quote comes after pages, not instead of them.

If you can only afford one professional pass, buy the highest-leverage stage for this draft. A cheap proofread on a structurally wobbly novel is not thrift. It is a professional-looking unfinished book.

Red flags that mean do not hire

  • Guarantees of agents, bestseller lists, Amazon ranking, or “we will make it sell.”
  • No sample, no written scope, and pressure to pay 100% up front with no milestones.
  • A rate that only makes sense if the manuscript is barely read.
  • Proofreading sold as developmental work, or “full edit” with no list of deliverables.
  • Confusion about who will actually read your pages.

Self-publishing removed the gatekeepers. It did not remove the need for cold eyes, and it did not make every listing trustworthy. Readers still abandon books that confuse, drag, or feel unprofessional by page ten. Hire someone who will tell you the truth about the page you have—not the career you want.

When you are ready, send the four facts (genre, words, deadline, stage) and we will answer like editors, not like a catalog. If the honest answer is that you should wait, you will hear that too.

FAQ

How do I hire a book editor for a self-published novel?

Name the stage (developmental, copyedit, or proof), send genre, word count, and a sample, then compare a written scope—not a star rating. Hire after serious self-revision. Polar Bear’s path is a fit check, sample pages, a written scope, then delivery and debrief. Start on the contact page with those four facts.

Should I hire a boutique editor or use a marketplace?

A marketplace is useful when you want a catalog of freelancers and you already know how to brief, sample, and contract. A boutique shop is useful when you want one editorial relationship, a named process, and someone who will tell you if you are buying the wrong pass. Neither is automatically better. Chemistry, scope, and stage-fit beat the storefront.

What should I send when I request a quote?

Genre, current word count, deadline if you have one, and where the manuscript stands (first draft, post-beta, story locked, or pre-upload). Attach or offer sample pages. That packet lets an editor recommend a stage instead of selling you the most expensive label.

How much should I budget to hire a book editor?

Plan two lines: a story-stage pass (critique or developmental) and a language-stage pass (copyedit, then proof). Educational bands often run about $0.03–$0.06 per word for developmental work and $0.018–$0.035 for copyediting. Use the cost calculator for your length, then request a scoped quote. Ranges are not a promise.

Can one editor handle developmental work and copyediting?

Sometimes, and never on the same frozen draft. Developmental work changes the book; copyediting polishes the book that results. If one editor does both, they should be sequential passes with your revision in between. Ask for separate scopes so you are not paying copyedit rates for structural advice, or the reverse.

Will hiring an editor get me an agent or guarantee sales?

No. A reputable editor will not promise traditional deals, bestseller lists, or sales. Editing improves the manuscript you put in front of readers, agents, or storefronts. The market still decides. Anyone guaranteeing placement is selling a different product.

Do I need a contract to hire a book editor?

Yes. A written scope should name deliverables, timeline, price, what happens if word count changes, and whether any revision round is included. Most trade manuscripts do not require an NDA; reputable editors treat files as confidential by default. No sample, no contract, and pressure to pay everything up front are reasons to walk.
