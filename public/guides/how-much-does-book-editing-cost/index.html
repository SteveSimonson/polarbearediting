How much does book editing cost in 2026?

Realistic book editing cost ranges for developmental editing, copyediting, and proofreading—plus 60k, 80k, and 100k ballparks, what changes the price, and how to budget.

Authors rarely overpay because rates exist. They overpay because they buy the wrong type of edit for their stage—or hire too early and pay twice. This guide gives educational market ranges for trade-length manuscripts, then shows you how to spend like a professional. None of it is a promise of agents, lists, or sales.

Quick answer: For an 80,000-word novel, educational indie-market bands typically run $640–$1,440 for a critique, $800–$1,600 for proofreading, $1,440–$2,800 for copyediting, $2,400–$4,800 for developmental editing, and $3,600–$6,400 for a developmental + copy package. These are not Polar Bear quotes.

How much does it cost to edit an 80,000-word novel?

Eighty thousand words is the planning number most commercial fiction and narrative nonfiction authors search. It is not a law—romance and thrillers often land shorter; fantasy and some nonfiction run longer—but it is the cleanest way to compare stickers.

Pass 80k ballpark Hire it when
Manuscript critique$640–$1,440You need a diagnosis, not a full structural partnership
Proofreading$800–$1,600The text is frozen, often after layout
Copyediting$1,440–$2,800Story is locked; language and consistency are next
Developmental editing$2,400–$4,800Structure, character, or argument still moves
Dev + copy package$3,600–$6,400You want a two-pass path scoped together

A full indie path—developmental, then revise, then copyedit, then proof—can stack. Budget the high-leverage pass first. Do not “save” by buying proofreading for a first draft. That is how rough books ship polished.

If you only remember one row: a genuine developmental edit on 80,000 words is usually a few thousand dollars, not a few hundred. A copyedit in the mid-four-figures is not automatically a better deal if the plot still leaks.

EFA-style rates, without pretending they are a quote

The Editorial Freelancers Association rate chart is a survey of what members report charging—medians, not a mandated price list, and not a Polar Bear invoice. Use it as climate, not weather.

What the EFA-style picture usually shows:

  • Proofreading is the lightest language pass and the cheapest genuine professional service.
  • Copyediting costs more because it carries a style sheet, consistency, and sentence-level judgment—not a spellcheck.
  • Developmental editing costs more still because the editor is thinking in structure, not only in commas.
  • Hourly vs per-word can describe the same job. Per-word is easier to compare across manuscripts; hourly is honest when the sample is messy.

Our tables sit in the indie and pre-agent overlap of that climate: low enough to be useful for self-publishing budgets, high enough that a “developmental edit” still has to mean structural work. If a posted rate only makes sense if the manuscript is barely read, it is not the same product—even when the label matches.

What moves the price

  • Word count — still the primary lever. 60k, 80k, and 100k are the columns authors actually compare.
  • Depth of intervention — light line clean-up vs multi-pass developmental work
  • Genre complexity — multi-POV epic fantasy and specialized nonfiction take longer
  • Manuscript condition — a rough draft costs more than a twice-revised book
  • Timeline — rush fees are real; planning is cheaper than panic

How to budget without regret

  1. Identify stage: structure still moving? Do not buy proofreading.
  2. Price the correct pass first (often critique or developmental). See developmental editing cost if that is the job.
  3. Reserve a second tranche for copyedit + proof after revisions. Copyediting cost is a language pass, not a plot fix.
  4. Compare sample pages, not only sticker rates.
Rule of thumb: If you can only afford one professional pass, choose the highest-leverage stage for your draft—not the cheapest label.

Posted stickers vs a scoped edit

Some sites are built to answer “what does copyediting cost at X words” with an instant number. That is useful when the manuscript is already story-locked. It is incomplete when the real question is “what does this book need?”

  • Editor World is a clear example of posted per-word copyediting and proofreading. Fine for a language pass. It does not replace a structural diagnosis.
  • Reedsy is a marketplace of independent editors. Rates vary by person; the listing is not the scope.
  • Scribendi and similar services are built for volume language work. Ask what you are actually buying.

A two-cent copyedit can be fair. It cannot do a developmental editor’s job. If betas are confused, bored, or lost, spend on structure first.

DIY that actually helps

Before you spend: complete a full self-revision, use beta readers, and read your work aloud. That preparation does not replace a pro—but it makes every paid hour worth more. See the self-publishing editing checklist.

FAQ

Is $0.01/word for developmental editing realistic?

Usually no—for a genuine structural edit. Ultra-low rates often mean a skim, outsourcing, or a mislabeled service. Ask for sample pages and a clear deliverable list.

Should I pay per word or flat fee?

Either can be fair if scope is written down. Per-word is transparent for length; flat fees work when the editor has assessed a sample and knows the work intensity.

Can I skip developmental and only copyedit?

Yes—if structure and story already work. If betas report confusion, boredom, or pacing issues, copyediting will polish problems you still need to fix.

Are these Editorial Freelancers Association rates or Polar Bear quotes?

Neither is a Polar Bear invoice. The tables use educational per-word bands for the indie and pre-agent market. EFA rate charts report survey medians for association members; they are guidance, not a price list. Use them to sanity-check a scope, then get sample pages.

Why do some websites quote copyediting near $0.021 per word?

Marketplace and mill rates often price a language pass at the low end, especially on slow turnaround. That can be fair for a clean, story-locked manuscript. It is not a developmental edit. If structure is still moving, a cheap copyedit is the expensive option.

Do I need both copyediting and proofreading?

If you are self-publishing, usually yes: copyedit while the text can still flex, then proof after layout. Combining them into one last-minute pass leaves layout errors and late rewrites uncaught.
