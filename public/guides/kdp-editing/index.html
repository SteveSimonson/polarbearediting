Do you need an editor before you upload to KDP?

Kindle Direct Publishing will take a file that would not survive a cold read. Editing before upload is a quality sequence—not a sales guarantee.

Kindle Direct Publishing removed the gatekeeper. It did not remove the reader. You can upload a first draft, a spellchecked draft, or a book that has been through developmental work, copyediting, interior layout, and a proof. The dashboard treats those files similarly. The Look Inside sample does not.

This guide is for indie authors who are about to put a book on Amazon and want a clear answer to three questions: do you need an editor, in what order should the work happen, and which late-stage jobs are not editing at all. Polar Bear will not promise ranking, reviews, or Kindle Unlimited pages. A professional pass only gives the manuscript a fair chance to be judged on the story.

What KDP actually checks (and what it ignores)

KDP cares whether your files meet technical specs, whether metadata is complete, and whether the content violates Amazon’s publishing guidelines. Those are real constraints. They are not a substitute for story judgment, a language pass, or a last error hunt.

You can publish without a professional editor. The store will still show a cover, a description, and a sample. That sample is the quality gate that matters. Separate two questions and you will spend less money in the wrong place:

  • Can I publish? Yes, if the file is valid and the content is allowed.
  • Is this the book I mean to put my name on? That is editorial.

Spellcheck, Kindle Create, and a friend who “has a good eye” are not a copyedit. Marketplace listings that sell a 48-hour “edit” for a novel-length manuscript are usually a skim. If you need a map of the jobs, start with types of book editing.

Do you need an editor before you upload?

You do not need one to press publish. You need one if you want professional-level structure, language, and consistency—and you cannot get there with self-revision and beta readers alone.

Hire when beta readers stall, contradict, or abandon the book; when you are too close to see the structure; when you have revised hard and still find new problems every pass; or when you are about to spend money on a cover and ads for a manuscript that has never had cold eyes.

Wait—or start smaller—when the draft is incomplete, you are still moving chapters, or you only want reassurance. Editing is expensive when you ignore the notes, and it is wasted when you buy proofreading for a book that still needs surgery. See when to hire an editor.

No editor, including Polar Bear, can promise that a KDP listing will sell. Editing reduces preventable reasons to bounce. Marketing, cover, category, price, and the book itself still have to do the rest.

The order of edits (this is how you avoid paying twice)

The wasteful KDP path is familiar: format a draft, “proof” the PDF, rewrite chapter four, format again, upload, notice the typos in Look Inside, panic. Each loop costs time and often a second invoice.

The sequence that holds:

  1. Finish a complete draft—not a perfect one.
  2. Cool off, then self-revise for structure, not commas.
  3. Use in-genre beta readers with specific questions.
  4. If the story or argument still wobbles, book a critique or developmental pass. Revise again.
  5. Copyedit once the content is locked.
  6. Format the ebook and print interiors.
  7. Proofread the near-final files.
  8. Upload quality control: front matter, chapter breaks, cover thumbnail, description.

If you can only buy one professional pass, buy the highest-leverage stage for this draft—not the cheapest label. Proofreading a structurally confused novel is a tidy disaster. The full sequence lives in the self-publishing editing checklist.

Polar Bear sequence for KDP: self → beta → developmental or critique → revise → copyedit → format → proof → publish. Skip a stage only when you can name why it is already done.

Formatting vs proofreading

Authors blur these because both happen “at the end.” They are different jobs, and mixing them is how interiors get expensive.

Formatting is production craft: readable type, hierarchy, stable navigation, print trim, ebook reflow. Polar Bear’s book formatting builds interiors meant to survive KDP and other stores. It does not rewrite your sentences or catch every typo.

Proofreading is the last error hunt: residual typos, punctuation slips, bad breaks, repeated words, and “how did that survive?” glitches. It belongs after copyediting, and preferably after layout, because formatting introduces its own errors—odd hyphens, missing scene breaks, a chapter title that jumped styles. See proofreading.

If you proof a Word file and then pour it into a template, you have not proofed the book readers will see. If you format first and then do a heavy copyedit, you will pay to reflow the interior. Freeze the text, then produce the files, then hunt leftovers.

What to freeze before anyone lays out the book

Before formatting starts, lock the title, subtitle, series name, author-name spelling, front matter, chapter titles, scene-break marks, and any images that must live in the text. A moving target multiplies cost. If you are still debating an epilogue, you are not in production.

KDP notes that are not editing

Kindle Create, Vellum, and similar tools are production software. They do not replace a copyeditor. Neither does the spellchecker in your word processor.

Print and ebook are related but not identical. A print PDF has headers, page numbers, and a trim size. A reflowable ebook has to survive font changes and small screens. If you only format one, you still need to check the other before you call the book done.

Download your own proof files from KDP. Flip every chapter. Check the sample on a phone. This is unpaid quality control, and it catches problems no one can see in the working document.

Metadata—categories, keywords, description—is marketing copy. Kindle Unlimited exclusivity is a business choice, not an editorial one. Neither changes the order of edits, and neither is a substitute for a developmental pass.

What editing will not do on Amazon

A professional pass will not place you on a list, fill pages-read, or promise a star rating. It will not decide your price or make a weak premise into a phenomenon. Polar Bear’s job is the manuscript and, when scoped, the interior files.

If the story still moves, start with a critique or developmental conversation. If the story holds, ask about copyediting, then formatting, then proofreading. Send genre, word count, and whether this is draft two, post-beta, or pre-upload. We will tell you which pass belongs now—even when the honest answer is “revise first.”

FAQ

Does Amazon require a professional editor for KDP?

No. Kindle Direct Publishing will accept a valid file that meets content guidelines. Amazon is not your editor. Readers still judge the Look Inside sample, so a professional pass is a quality decision, not a store requirement.

Can I skip developmental editing and just proofread before upload?

Only if structure, pacing, and argument already work. Proofreading catches residual errors on a stable text. It will not fix a sagging middle, a confused timeline, or a narrator who never arrives at the point.

Should I format the book before or after proofreading?

Copyedit first, then format, then proof the laid-out files. Formatting introduces its own errors. Proofing a Word draft and pouring it into a template later means you have not proofed the book readers will see.

Will hiring an editor guarantee KDP sales or reviews?

No. Editing removes preventable reasons to bounce. It does not rank the listing, fill Kindle Unlimited pages, or promise star ratings. Cover, description, categories, price, and the book itself still have to earn the rest.

What file should I upload to KDP after editing?

Upload production files, not the working manuscript. That usually means a reflowable ebook and a print-ready PDF at your trim size, built after the copyedit is implemented. Then download KDP’s own proofs and check every chapter break before you hit publish.
