Copyediting that protects voice and precision

Line-level book copyediting for grammar, clarity, continuity, and style-sheet consistency after your structure is locked.

Copyediting is where professionalism becomes invisible. Readers should never notice the work—only that the prose is clean, consistent, and trustworthy. The red pen here is an instrument, not a personality transplant.

The process

  1. Fit check. Confirm the story or argument is actually locked. If Chapter 12 is still in surgery, we will send you back a stage rather than charge you twice.
  2. Sample pages. You see how we query voice, dialect, and intentional fragments—not only how we treat commas.
  3. Written scope. Light, medium, or heavy copyedit; whether a line-edit blend is included; style authority (Chicago, house, your sheet); timeline and word-count rules.
  4. The pass. Tracked changes in Word (or equivalent markup), queries in comments, a living style sheet as decisions accumulate.
  5. Handoff. You accept, reject, and answer queries. A proofread comes later—after you implement, and preferably after layout.

Unsure whether you need line work, copy work, or both? Read line editing vs copyediting. Still deciding if structure is truly done? Developmental vs copyediting.

What copyediting covers

  • Grammar, usage, punctuation, and syntax
  • Clarity and concision without flattening voice
  • Continuity: names, timelines, eye color, world rules
  • Style sheet: hyphenation, capitalization, spelling variants
  • Light fact flags when something smells off (not full fact-checking unless scoped)

Who it is for

Fiction, memoir, and narrative nonfiction authors whose big pieces already stand—indie, hybrid, or preparing pages for an agent. You want a professional language pass before formatting, upload, or submission, and you are willing to answer queries rather than expect silent rewrites.

It is also for sequel writers who need a style sheet that survives the next book: the same em dash rules, the same invented-term caps, the same character-name spellings.

What you receive

  • Tracked-changes manuscript (Word) or equivalent markup
  • Project style sheet you can reuse for sequels
  • Query comments where authorial choice is required
  • A short note on patterns (comma splices, viewpoint slips, continuity snags) so you can catch them next time

What it does not cover — and when not to buy it

Plot overhauls, chapter reordering, or “make this more commercial.” That is developmental editing. Doing copyedit too early means paying twice when scenes move.

  • Do not buy copyediting while you are still rewriting the ending or swapping POVs
  • Do not buy it as a cheaper developmental edit; clean sentences will not hide a sagging middle
  • Do not buy it if you only need a last typo hunt after layout—that is proofreading
  • Do not buy it if you want the editor to ghost the book into a different voice

If trusted readers still report confusion, boredom, or pacing issues, start with a critique or developmental pass. Timing guide: when to hire an editor.

FAQ

Copyedit vs proofread?

Copyediting improves language and consistency while the text can still flex. Proofreading is the last error hunt after everything—including often layout—is frozen.

Is copyediting the same as line editing?

Line editing prioritizes style, rhythm, and voice at the sentence level. Copyediting prioritizes correctness and consistency. Many book projects blend both; labels vary by editor—ask for a sample and a written scope.

How much does copyediting cost?

Educational market ranges for copyediting typically run $0.018–$0.035 per word. Proofreading is lower ($0.01–$0.02); developmental work is higher ($0.03–$0.06). Use the cost calculator for a ballpark, then request a scoped quote.

Will a copyedit change my voice?

It should not flatten it. We query authorial choices—dialect, fragments, rhythm—rather than silently rewriting you into workshop generic. If a sample sands the voice off the page, that is the wrong desk.

Should I copyedit before querying or uploading?

Yes, if structure and story already work. Agents and readers both trip on sloppy pages. If betas still report confusion, boredom, or pacing issues, copyediting will polish problems you still need to fix—buy developmental or a critique first.
