Manuscript critiques with actionable direction

A professional big-picture manuscript critique: strengths, risks, and a prioritized revision plan—without full copy markup.

Sometimes you do not need 3,000 margin notes. You need a sharp diagnostic: what is working, what is costing you readers, and which three revision moves matter most. A critique is that cold-light letter—warm enough that you can still pick up the pen afterward.

The process

  1. Fit check. Genre, word count, stage, and what you think is weak. If the book actually needs commas, not a diagnosis, we will say so.
  2. Sample pages. Opening plus a middle stretch, so you can hear how we talk about your work before you buy the full letter.
  3. Written scope. Letter length and sections, whether a short call is included, timeline, and the word-count the fee assumes.
  4. The read. The complete manuscript as a reader first, then as a structuralist. We are looking for the highest-leverage problems, not a catalog of every quibble.
  5. Delivery. The letter, a ranked revision sequence, and—if scoped—a debrief so you leave knowing fire one, fire two, fire three.

Who it is for

  • Authors between drafts who want a professional read before a full developmental edit
  • Writers preparing agent submissions who need opening-strength and overall coherence feedback—not a promise of representation
  • Budget-conscious projects that still deserve expert eyes
  • Writers whose beta readers contradict each other and who need a ranked list, not more opinions

Fiction, memoir, and narrative nonfiction. If you already know the book needs scene-level partnership and you have the developmental budget, skip the letter and go straight to developmental editing.

What you receive

A substantial editorial letter covering premise, structure, character/voice, pacing, and market-facing notes when relevant—plus a prioritized revision sequence. Not a vibe. Not a pile of unranked complaints.

  • What is already earning the reader’s time
  • Where the book leaks (stakes, causality, viewpoint, sagging middle, false ending)
  • Which revisions to do first so you do not rewrite in circles
  • An honest note if the next paid pass should be developmental, copy, or “revise and return”

Want deeper collaboration afterward? Critiques often convert cleanly into scoped developmental editing. They do not include tracked-changes copyediting. That is a different hire, after the big pieces stand.

When not to buy this

  • The draft is incomplete, or you already plan to throw out half of it
  • You want line edits, grammar, and a style sheet—buy copyediting after structure locks
  • You want a last typo hunt—that is proofreading, and only after the text is frozen
  • You already know you need full developmental markup and you are ready to fund it
  • You want praise, not a map. Hire when you are willing to act.

Best time: after a real self-revision, ideally after betas, and before you spend copyedit money on pages that may still move. See when to hire an editor.

FAQ

Is a manuscript critique the same as a developmental edit?

No. A critique is a diagnostic letter: strengths, risks, and a prioritized revision sequence. A developmental edit goes deeper, with more granular guidance and, when scoped, in-manuscript comments. Many authors use a critique to test fit before a full developmental budget.

How much does a manuscript critique cost?

Educational market ranges for a manuscript critique typically run $0.008–$0.018 per word—lower than developmental editing ($0.03–$0.06). Use the cost calculator for a ballpark, then request a scoped quote.

Will I get tracked changes?

Not on a standard critique. The deliverable is the letter, not line-by-line markup. If you need commas and consistency marked, that is copyediting after the big pieces stand. If you need scene-level comments throughout, that is a developmental scope.

Is a critique enough before self-publishing?

Sometimes, if the letter says the story already works and you still budget a copyedit and proof. A critique is not a substitute for language work or a last error hunt. If the letter names structural surgery, revise—then decide whether you need a full developmental pass.

How long is the editorial letter?

Long enough to be useful and short enough to act on—typically a substantial letter covering premise, structure, character or voice, pacing, and a ranked revision sequence, not a three-sentence vibe check.
